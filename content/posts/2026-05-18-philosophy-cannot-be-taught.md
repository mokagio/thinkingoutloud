title: 'Philosophy Cannot Be Taught'
---

In _The Count of Monte Cristo_ (Le Comte de Monte-Cristo) by Alexandre Dumas, the protagonist, Edmond Dantès, is astounded when his newfound learned friend Abbé Faria estimates it would take a couple of years for Dantès to learn all the disciplines and languages the Abbé is so well versed in.

> "Two years!" exclaimed Dantès; "do you really believe I can acquire all these things in so short a time?"

But there is an important caveat. To learn is not to know:

> "Not their application, certainly, but their principles you may; to learn is not to know; there are the learners and the learned. Memory makes the one, philosophy the other."

So, if philosophy makes the learned, how does one learn philosophy, Dantès asks.
The Abbé's reply:

> Philosophy cannot be taught; it is the application of the sciences to truth;

Philosophy is practical.
It cannot be taught, it can only be learned.
One learns philosophy by applying it, or rather by attempting to apply it.

It takes a few hours to read a philosophy book.
It might take a lifetime to master its lessons.
