title: Could The Way To Peace Of Mind Be Subtractive?
---

> ‘Minimize unhappiness’ is not just a negative formulation of the Utilitarian maxim ‘Maximize happiness’.
> There is a logical asymmetry here:
> we do not know how to make people happy, but we do know ways of lessening their unhappiness.
> — [_Popper_](https://www.amazon.com/Popper-Modern-Masters-Bryan-Magee/dp/0006860087), Bryan Magee

> It is far easier to figure out if something is fragile than to predict the occurrence of an event that may harm it.
> Fragility can be measured; risk is not measurable (outside of casinos or the minds of people who call themselves “risk experts”).
> — [_Antifragile_](https://www.amazon.com/Antifragile-Things-That-Disorder-Incerto/dp/0812979680), Nassim Taleb

At a time in which I feel swept away by external forces and unable to stop worrying about the future, Popper and Taleb's work cast a rope I can use to pull myself out of the maelstrom.

I can't predict the best career move, but I can identify points of fragility.

I may not be able to affect the outside forces that disrupt my peace of mind, but I can try to minimize the contact points, such as by opting out of political commentary podcasts.

As Leidy Klotz observes in his book [_Subtract_](https://www.amazon.com/Subtract-Untapped-Science-Leidy-Klotz/dp/1250249864), we tend to attempt to solve problems by adding things, but subtracting can often be a better option.

Maybe the way to peace of mind is one of subtracting stressors, be they sources of unhappiness or points of fragility.
